Frequently asked questions

TEHTER Technical Details

Does TEHTER require replacing our existing CAM nesting or ERP software?

No. TEHTER is designed as a non-invasive intelligence layer. It ingests export files (such as standard DXF, NC cutting logs, CSV, or XML reports) and cross-references them against your ERP purchase orders without modifying your existing machinery controllers.

How does TEHTER handle child-parts nested inside larger parent assemblies?

TEHTER maps parent BOM structures to their child components automatically. If a chassis assembly requires 4 gussets and 2 brackets per unit, TEHTER computes the aggregated total required across the entire batch and flags single missing child components.

Can TEHTER run on-premise without exposing CAD drawings to the cloud?

Yes. We provide complete air-gapped container deployments for industrial clients with sensitive proprietary designs or defense-grade IP requirements.

What formats are generated when an audit finishes?

TEHTER produces structured PDF audit certificates for shipping batches, alongside JSON, CSV, and automated ERP webhook updates for material planning and reorder queues.

Case study · Computer Vision & Attendance

Face ID & Liveness Detection

Face ID is the tensorixAI attendance module that recognises an employee from a camera and checks that the person is really present, not a photo. Employees register once from a short guided video, then check in by looking at the camera and following a random blink or head-turn challenge. It runs on the company’s own server, with employee data and photos kept in company-controlled storage.

The problem

Attendance had to be tied to a real, present person

Attendance had to be tied to a real, present person, and adding a new person had to stay simple.

  • Attendance could be marked for someone else. A friend could punch in for an absent employee.
  • A photo fooled face recognition. A held-up picture of an employee was still recognised as that person.
  • New joiners needed model training. Retraining the model for every new employee was not acceptable.
  • Public face data does not match real use. Public sets are zoomed-in static pictures, while real use varies in distance, angle and lighting.
  • Collecting employee photos was awkward. Asking staff for photos was impractical; a short video of each person was easier.
  • Different data needs different storage. Names and IDs belong in a database, while photos and videos belong in file storage.
  • Face data is sensitive. Biometric data had to stay under company control, and a failed check had to say why.
The solution

Liveness first, then identity

A check-in passes through a one-time liveness challenge, five rule-based checks and an anti-spoof model before the face is matched to an employee. Enrollment is a separate path, so a new person needs no training.

Guided enrollment

Employees register once from a short guided video: look straight, turn left and right, look up and down, and move closer. Only sharp frames with one clear face are kept, so a new employee needs no model training.

Face fingerprints

The system finds the face and turns it into a numerical fingerprint that is matched by similarity. Identification works by comparison, so adding a person never retrains anything.

One-time liveness challenge

The server issues a random challenge, either blink twice or turn the head left and right, valid for 30 seconds. A photo cannot blink or turn, and a challenge cannot be reused.

Five rule-based checks

Enough clear frames, same person throughout, blink count, a head turn of at least 10 degrees each side, and a depth check that tells a 3D head from a flat sheet. Every decision shows which check failed.

Anti-spoof model

An anti-spoof model scores every frame as live or spoof, adding evidence beyond movement alone.

Verified check-in only

Attendance is stored only after liveness passes and the face matches an enrolled employee, with a 5-minute cooldown to stop double punches.

Data and photos stored apart

A database holds names, IDs and fingerprints, while file storage holds enrollment photos in one folder per employee, so fingerprints can be rebuilt with another model.

Simple integration

A single service handles enrollment, challenges, check-in and employee records, so the interface can work without knowing the models, and automated tests cover the logic.

Case study · Conversational AI & Manufacturing

Multilingual AI Chatbot Assistant

The AI Chatbot Assistant is a voice and text assistant that lets staff ask questions of the live company database in six languages and draft purchase documents by conversation. It runs on a local language model, so answers need no cloud service.

The problem

Useful data, but out of reach

Useful data sat in the database and in documents, but only people with technical skills, and the right language, could reach it.

  • Answers needed technical query skills. Staff could not ask about parts, procurement or production on their own.
  • Floor and office staff speak different languages. English-only tools excluded Hindi and Tamil speakers.
  • Typing is impractical on the shop floor. There was no hands-free way to ask or listen.
  • Information locked in documents. PDFs, spreadsheets, images and recordings could not be queried in conversation.
  • Slow manual drafting of purchase documents. PRs, POs and GRNs were written by hand.
  • Raw rows are hard to read. Results needed to become charts.
  • Data privacy and running cost. A cloud model would send company data outside and bill per use.
  • A fixed tool never improves. Wrong answers repeated with no learning from feedback.
The solution

Ask in any language, act only on a yes

Each message passes through language detection and an intent router, then takes one of three paths: a read-only database query, a confirmed write action, or a general reply. Writes are kept entirely apart from the query path.

Plain-language database questions

An AI agent writes a single read-only database query from a snapshot of the database structure, validated twice, and answers use only the returned rows. Staff get plain-language access, and the read path cannot modify data.

One assistant, six languages

Language is detected automatically, covering English, Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam, with answer rules for each language and script.

Ask and listen without typing

Spoken questions are transcribed by speech-to-text, and replies can be spoken back in a cloned voice, with a neural-voice fallback.

Chat with your documents

Upload documents, spreadsheets, presentations, images, audio, video or ZIP files (read up to 8,000 characters) and they become context for the next question.

Purchase documents by conversation

Separate handlers draft a purchase requisition, a purchase order from an existing quotation, and a goods receipt note against a PO. Nothing is written until the user says yes.

Charts on request

A visualisation agent builds chart panels from query results or uploaded tables.

Runs on local infrastructure

The language model, speech recognition and retrieval store all run locally, so no cloud model is needed. Only email sending, the fallback voice and web scraping use the internet.

Learns from feedback

Every turn is logged. Ratings of 4 or 5 feed a store of good question-to-query examples, and preference pairs can drive an optional fine-tune, so accuracy improves with use.

Case study · Engineering Data & Manufacturing

Laser Parts Database

The Laser Parts Database turns spec-review spreadsheets and DXF drawings into a searchable database, with an AI image fingerprint for every drawing so similar parts can be found across projects. It replaced an earlier prototype screen with a plain web interface, and nine projects have been reconciled with it.

The problem

Scattered data, no link to the drawing

Engineering data was scattered across spec sheets and drawing folders, and nothing linked a drawing to its part number or warned about repeat cutting.

  • Spec data and drawings lived in separate files. There was no fast way to find the drawing for a part number.
  • No way to find similar parts. Engineers could not check existing parts across projects before redoing work.
  • Messy inputs. DXF names carried suffixes such as dxf001 or rev, the same name appeared in different sub-folders, and spreadsheet headers varied with title blocks above the table.
  • Accidental double cutting. A part already nested and cut could be downloaded again without anyone approving it.
  • Re-imports risked duplicates. Projects had to be loaded again as specs changed.
  • Data problems found on real projects. Mismatched data sizes, missing uniqueness rules, overwritten records and wrong child-part numbers surfaced on real data.
  • Non-technical users needed a simple screen. The first interface was replaced by a plain web page anyone could use.
The solution

Every drawing linked, every repeat approved

A user creates a project, uploads its spec sheet and points the app at a DXF folder; the app imports parts, renders drawings, matches them to part numbers and stores an AI fingerprint for each.

Spec import with linked drawings

Spec sheets are imported with automatic header detection. DXFs are rendered to images, matched by filename and stored with their raw files and an AI fingerprint, so every drawing is linked to its part or child part.

Find similar parts

Each drawing gets an AI image fingerprint stored for search, so similar parts can be found across projects before work is redone.

Tolerant filename matching

Matching tries exact names first, then variants that ignore separators, prefixes and revision suffixes, scoped to the project. A dedupe check finds clashes before ingestion, so fewer files go unmatched and nothing is silently overwritten.

Re-download approval

A user requests part numbers, the manager gets an email with a decision link, and approved parts become downloadable. Duplicate cutting runs need a logged manager decision.

Safe re-imports

Imports update existing records instead of duplicating them, and a reset-and-reingest option clears a project in a safe order, so specs can be reloaded as they change.

Reconciliation reports

Backfill and reconciliation tools produce a report per project. Nine projects have been reconciled with per-file match reports.

A simple interface

A plain web interface with three tabs, a live progress bar and background jobs lets staff use it without a terminal.

Case study · Procurement & Manufacturing

Purchase Management Pipeline

The Purchase module is a complete purchase pipeline, from purchase request to payment voucher. It began as a tool that scraped steel prices from an online supplier marketplace and now replaces the email chain with one gated, fully audited process, with a web app, dashboards, one-click email approvals and background reminders.

The problem

A purchase process that ran on email and memory

Eight problems drove the module; the common thread is a purchase process that ran on email and memory, with no enforced order and no audit trail.

  • Approvals depended on management opening the system. Decisions were slow, and there was no record of who approved what.
  • Suppliers chosen on quoted price alone. Quotes mixed units (per tonne vs per piece) and ignored freight to the plant, so the cheapest quote was not the cheapest delivered cost.
  • Requests and POs left waiting. Purchase requests sat unanswered, and POs stayed open for weeks with no prompt.
  • Receipts, bills and payments not tied to the PO. Differences between ordered, billed and received quantities stayed hidden.
  • No formal path for rejected material. There was no standard return to Accounts for a debit note.
  • Wrong PO weights fixed by overwriting. The original PO was lost and corrections left no history.
  • Stock followed the ordered quantity. Stock figures did not match what physically arrived.
  • The prototype stood apart from company data. It ran on a separate local database, so a goods receipt note could not point at a real PO line.
The solution

One gated pipeline, every step audited

One gated pipeline replaces the email chain: purchase request, Gate 1, market search and shortlist, Gate 2, RFQ, quotations, Gate 3, automatic PO PDF, then goods receipt, bill and payment. Gate order is enforced in the system itself, not only on the screens.

One-click email approvals

Signed Approve and Reject links arrive by email, and every decision is written to an approval log, so management can approve from a phone, fully audited.

Cost-based supplier shortlist

Supplier prices are gathered from an online marketplace, units are normalised, results are filtered by material and thickness, and road distance to the plant is added. Management sees a ranked top-5 shortlist by landed cost.

Automatic escalation

A request left unanswered for 24 hours is escalated (checked every 30 minutes), and a daily reminder goes out for POs open 7 days or more, so delays surface automatically.

Receipts, bills and payments tied to the PO

A goods receipt workflow with mismatch detection, supplier bill entry showing ordered-vs-billed differences, payment vouchers, and PR, PO and GRN PDFs.

Purchase returns

A purchase return is created in the same step as the goods receipt rejection and forwarded to Accounts, so a return cannot exist without its rejection.

Append-only PO amendments

The effective value is the original plus its amendments, leaving the original PO untouched with a full correction history.

Stock that matches receipts

A single route updates stock from goods receipts, using accepted quantity and received weight, so stock matches what physically arrived, with movement history.

Built on company data

All modules run on the company’s own database, and quotation approval creates a tax-aware PO with one line per quotation line.

Case study · Operations AI & Manufacturing

AI-Driven Capacity Planning

Knowing what the week looks like before it starts. Every business that makes things to order faces the same question at the start of the week: can we really deliver what we have promised? A planning assistant replaces that guesswork with a plan that is visible weeks ahead, grounded in what actually happens on the floor, and easy for planners to trust.

The challenge

The plan lives in a spreadsheet and in people’s heads

An order arrives, someone estimates each step from experience, a planner slots the job in, and someone checks the materials. When a machine stops or a rush order lands, the plan is rebuilt by hand, and what actually happened stays on paper job cards and never reaches the next estimate.

  • Estimates vary. Two people will price and schedule the same job differently.
  • Changeovers are invisible. Time spent switching between jobs is rarely planned, so it shows up as idle time.
  • Materials are checked too late. Jobs reach a machine with nothing to work on.
  • People and machines are planned separately. A free machine may have nobody qualified to run it, so overtime becomes the default fix.
  • Nobody can see beyond the current week. Delivery dates and quotes are given without checking capacity, costing hours of re-planning, bottlenecks found after jobs have queued up, and dates customers learn not to trust.
The solution

The planner stays in charge, the assistant does the sums

A planning assistant sits alongside the systems a business already uses. It reads orders, stock, shift patterns and past job times and turns them into a clear picture of the next few weeks: where the pinch points are, and what could be done about them. It does not replace the planner.

1. Collect

Orders, stock, shifts and past job times.

2. Estimate

How long each job will really take.

3. Compare

Work coming in against capacity, week by week.

4. Suggest

A schedule, plus costed options if overloaded.

5. Approve and learn

The planner decides, and actual times feed back in.

Three time horizons

The right question at each horizon

Next quarter

Will forecast work exceed what our machines and teams can deliver? Do we need an extra shift, new equipment or an outside partner?

Next 2 to 4 weeks

Which weeks and which areas are overloaded? Should we use overtime, move jobs or outsource?

Today

In what order should each machine run its jobs?

What makes this approach different

Planning you can trust

Capacity means people as well as machinesAn area can only deliver the lower of what its machines and its qualified team can do, so a plan that counts machines alone overstates what the business can achieve.
A quick answer to “can we take this order?”Sales can check capacity before promising a date, and get a realistic range rather than one hopeful number.
Estimates come with a rangeInstead of “40 minutes”, the planner sees “most likely 40, almost certainly under 48”, so the busiest machine gets a sensible buffer.
Every suggestion is explainedPlanners can see why a job was placed where it was, and nothing reaches the floor until they approve it.
It learns from the floorActual times flow back in, so estimates improve week by week. Until there is enough history, it relies on simple, transparent rules.
Expected result: faster planning, earlier warning of overload and delivery dates customers can trust, to be measured against a baseline in a pilot.
What the planner sees

Five simple views, one clear week

A capacity overview, a machine-by-machine timeline, a list of jobs at risk and why, a what-if screen for rush orders and breakdowns, and an estimate review that sets the system’s range beside the planner’s own number. Here is the capacity overview for one week, using sample data.

How success would be measured

A baseline first, then before and after

In a pilot, each of the following would be recorded as a baseline before go-live and compared afterwards.

  • Planner hours per weekly plan and per re-plan
  • Estimate accuracy: planned minutes against actual minutes
  • Utilisation and queue time at the busiest work area
  • Changeover time per shift
  • Overtime, outsourcing and expediting cost
  • On-time delivery and quote turnaround

Results would be reported as before and after, with the length of the period and number of orders, and any seasonal effect noted. Because on-time delivery also depends on suppliers, the system would be credited only for what it controls.

Getting started

About 20 weeks, starting small

A typical engagement runs about 20 weeks with a core team of four, and starts small: one site and the one or two work areas that matter most.

Discovery and data audit2 weeks. Map data sources, agree what success means and record the baseline.
Data foundations4 weeks. Connect the systems, tidy the data and set up a simple way to capture actual job times.
Estimates and forecasts4 weeks. Build and test job-time and workload predictions against actuals and the planners’ own estimates.
Scheduler and dashboards6 weeks. Release the capacity overview first, then the schedule and what-if screens, reviewed with planners at each step.

What we need from you: order and routing history, stock and purchase dates, shift calendars and team skills, and a way to record when each job starts and stops (a tablet or barcode scan is enough). Actual job times are the one thing most businesses lack, so capturing them is part of the first phase.

Honest limits

What to expect

  • Accuracy depends on recorded actual times. Without them, the system starts on simple rules and improves as data builds up.
  • Forecasts several weeks out are indicative when order history is short.
  • Work that depends heavily on individual skill, such as hand finishing, is harder to predict than machine-paced work.
  • Unrecorded workarounds cannot be modelled, and results in one business will not carry over automatically to another.

Case study · Computer Vision & Manufacturing

AI-Driven Weld Quality Inspection

Welding is fundamental to structural steel fabrication, heavy machinery, automotive assembly and energy pipelines. Weld integrity directly determines load capacity, fatigue endurance and operational safety, so rigorous quality verification is essential before commissioning. AI image analysis replaces subjective manual checks with automated, real-time visual and non-destructive evaluation, giving complete and consistent coverage at line speed.

The challenge

Weld defects are costly, and checks come too late

Traditional quality control relies on manual visual testing and post-process non-destructive testing such as X-ray and ultrasonic methods. Undetected defects lead to expensive gouging, re-welding, schedule delays and high downstream scrap costs.

  • Subjective results and fatigue. Human inspectors tire over long shifts, so detection rates are inconsistent.
  • Labour shortages. A shortage of certified NDT technicians limits inspection capacity.
  • Delayed rework. Offline inspections happen hours or days after welding, creating bottlenecks and heavy rework costs.
  • Difficult imaging conditions. Surface cameras face glare from fresh metal, roughness, spatter and oils, while X-ray images are low-contrast and noisy, making harmless thickness changes hard to tell from critical internal flaws.
  • High annotation cost. Training AI models needs large datasets labelled by certified NDT experts.
  • Classification complexity. Minor surface irregularities must be told apart from severe structural cracks.
  • Compliance needs. Industry standards require human-in-the-loop oversight for safety and legal compliance.
What a weld failure looks like

Six defects worth catching early

Sub-optimal heating, joint misalignments, contaminated shielding gas or operator errors introduce critical structural flaws. In high-risk environments a single weld failure can mean severe environmental damage, safety hazards and significant financial loss.

Porosity

Trapped gas bubbles create voids that weaken cross-sectional strength and concentrate stress.

Incomplete fusion

Poor bonding between weld and parent metal creates internal gaps that expand under load.

Lack of penetration

Weld metal fails to reach the root of the joint, lowering load capacity and fatigue resistance.

Cracks

High internal stresses cause thermal cracking, posing immediate structural hazards.

Undercut

Grooves melted into the base metal at the weld toe create stress points that accelerate fatigue.

Slag inclusions

Non-metallic material trapped inside the weld disrupts structural continuity.

The solution

Real-time AI, with an inspector in the loop

We propose a multi-modal AI approach that reads surface camera feeds and X-ray scans. Neural networks evaluate the images in real time and route flagged regions to a central decision support system with human-in-the-loop verification.

Surface image analysis

High-resolution cameras with custom lighting on robotic welding cells evaluate video in real time, detecting undercut, porosity, burn-through and excessive spatter during or right after welding. Image processing clears metallic glare and reflections.

X-ray image analysis

Digitized X-ray scans are analysed at pixel level to map internal porosity, slag channels, lack of root penetration and microscopic cracks, with contrast normalisation to highlight subtle flaws against dense metal.

Instant analysis at the workstation

Processing units at the workstation analyse incoming images on the spot, so results arrive while the weld is still fresh.

Confidence scoring

Overlays mark flagged areas with dynamic confidence ratings, so inspectors see where to look and how sure the system is.

Risk-based triage

High-confidence clear welds pass automatically. Defective or low-confidence regions are routed straight to human inspectors.

Inspector feedback loop

Certified technicians confirm or override the AI’s predictions, and rejected welds automatically send parameter adjustments back to the welding controllers.

Phased roadmap

Start with the surface, then go inside

Deployment is structured across two phases to minimise operational risk and validate performance early.

Phase 1: Surface visual inspection pilot

Real-time surface defect checks using cameras with custom lighting, covering undercut, spatter, surface cracks and bead width variance. Processing happens at the workstation, with line operator review. Low risk, fast feedback.

Phase 2: X-ray inspection integration

Internal volumetric flaw evaluation from digitized X-ray scans, covering internal porosity, lack of penetration and slag inclusions. Processing runs on a local server or private cloud, with Level II/III NDT inspector sign-off. Managed risk, strict compliance.

Surface inspection can be deployed rapidly on standard hardware. X-ray integration needs additional preparation, including radiation safety controls, secure handling of scan data and expert-validated training data.

Case study · Predictive Maintenance & Manufacturing

Predictive Maintenance Platform

Spotting machine trouble early, and saying why. A bearing rarely fails overnight. Weeks before it does, the machine usually sounds a little different, runs a little warmer or pulls a little more current. Most plants only find out when it stops. This platform watches for those early changes, tells the maintenance team what it thinks is going wrong, and gets the repair into the schedule while there is still time to prepare.

The challenge

The sensors work, the follow-through doesn’t

Plenty of plants have tried condition monitoring and quietly given up on it. The sensors usually work. The trouble comes after: readings are judged against one fixed limit, nobody can tell what an alert means, and the maintenance plan never hears about it.

  • Fixed alarm limits. One threshold for every operating state, so a pump at half load or any start-up trips it and people stop looking.
  • Alerts with no explanation. A health score or an “anomaly” flag leaves engineers to work out the cause themselves.
  • Few failures to learn from. A well-run plant has very few breakdowns, so there are not enough examples to train fault or remaining-life models.
  • Faulty sensors. Loose mounts and flat-lined or clipped channels make a bad sensor look like a fault, or hide a real one.
  • Alerts that ignore maintenance reality. Nobody checks stock, recent repairs or who is free, and a warning is no use if the part arrives after the failure date.
  • Thin repair records. Closed work orders say a job was done, not what was found, so there is no way to learn from past alerts or measure them.

The cost shows up as emergency repairs and rush freight, as false alarms that teach teams to ignore the system, and as diagnosis done by hand because no alert came with evidence. And since confirmed findings are never recorded, none of it gets better over time.

The solution

A second pair of eyes on every machine

The platform sits beside your maintenance and control systems and learns what a healthy pump, motor or gearbox looks like at different speeds, loads and times of year. When a machine drifts away from that, it checks several signals together to work out the likely cause, shows its evidence, and raises a work order. It only advises.

1. Sensors feed a local unit

Sensors on each machine feed a small unit next to it. Speed and load come from existing machine controls, and weather from the site.

2. A first check at the machine

The unit boils raw signals down to a few hundred numbers a minute and does a quick first check. If the network drops, it keeps going and sends the backlog later.

3. It learns what normal looks like

An AI model trained only on healthy data learns each machine’s normal for its speed, load and weather. One model per machine type is tuned on each machine’s own data, so a new asset does not have to wait for a failure to be useful.

4. Signals are read together

Vibration plus ultrasound points to early bearing or gear damage. Current plus temperature points to winding or insulation trouble. Power against output points to drag or poor lubrication.

5. Three alert levels

Watch, plan and act. A change has to hold for a while before it counts, and alerts are held back during start-up and just after a repair. Each alert shows what moved and how it compares with the machine’s own past. Remaining life is given only for machine types with real run-to-failure history.

6. Work order and learning loop

A work order goes to the maintenance system with the diagnosis, the spare-part status and the skill level needed. When the job closes, the engineer notes what was found, or why it was a false alarm. That feeds retraining, and a person signs off every model update.

What we look at

Machine conditionVibration on three axes, ultrasound and acoustic emission, bearing and casing temperature. Early signs of bearing wear, gear damage and poor lubrication.
PerformanceMotor current, power drawn against output, speed and load. Electrical faults and mechanical drag.
Operating contextStart-up and shutdown, duty cycle, the product being run, recent maintenance. Tells normal changes apart from real faults.
EnvironmentAmbient temperature, humidity, air pressure. Separates a hot afternoon from a hot bearing.
RecordsFailure history, past repair findings, asset age, spare stock and lead times. Puts each alert in context and shows what can actually be done.
Sensor healthFlat-lines, clipping, drift, dropouts. A broken sensor raises a sensor alert, not a machine alert.

Oil analysis and thermal cameras can be added later, for the assets that justify the extra hardware.

Cut once

Identical adjacent parts are consolidated and shared edges detected, so parts and common edges are cut only once.
